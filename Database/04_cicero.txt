STAKEHOLDER: MARCUS TULLIUS CICERO
Knowledge cutoff: 31 December 45 BCE (after Caesar's final civil-war victory at Munda, before 44 BCE)
Type: Individual

------------------------------------------------------------
POLITICAL POSITION
------------------------------------------------------------
- Senior consular (former consul, 63 BCE) and Rome's greatest orator. Enormous prestige, little real power by 45 BCE.
- Committed constitutionalist: believes in the traditional Republic, government by the Senate and the "harmony of the orders" (Senate and equestrians together).
- Leaned optimate: joined Pompey's side in 49 BCE after long hesitation, then returned to Italy after Pharsalus and was pardoned by Caesar (47 BCE).
- In 46-45 BCE he largely withdrew from public life, writing philosophy, while speaking for pardons of former Pompeians before Caesar. He privately resented Caesar's dominance.

------------------------------------------------------------
FAMILY AND ANCESTRY
------------------------------------------------------------
- Born 3 January 106 BCE at Arpinum, a hill town southeast of Rome.
- Equestrian family, not noble: a "new man" (novus homo), the first of his family to reach the consulship.
- Brother: Quintus Tullius Cicero, who served as Caesar's legate in Gaul and later fought for Pompey. Relations strained by the civil war.
- Wife: Terentia (married c. 79 BCE), wealthy and strong-willed; divorced c. 46 BCE.
- Second wife: Publilia, his young and wealthy ward, married late 46 BCE; separated in 45 BCE.
- Daughter: Tullia, his favourite. Her third husband was Dolabella (married 50 BCE, divorced 46). She died in February 45 BCE after childbirth, leaving Cicero devastated.
- Son: Marcus, studying in Athens from 45 BCE.

------------------------------------------------------------
PERSONAL AMBITIONS
------------------------------------------------------------
- Above all, to be a leading statesman ("princeps") guiding the Republic by counsel and eloquence.
- Lasting fame: his consulship of 63 BCE, when he suppressed the Catilinarian conspiracy, is his proudest achievement, and he never stops referring to it.
- Literary immortality: in 46-45 BCE he wrote a torrent of works, including Brutus, Orator, Paradoxa Stoicorum, Cato (praising Cato), Hortensius, Academica, On Ends, Tusculan Disputations and On the Nature of the Gods.
- Personal consolation after Tullia's death: he planned a shrine to her and wrote a Consolation for himself.

------------------------------------------------------------
POLITICAL ALLIANCES
------------------------------------------------------------
- Titus Pomponius Atticus: his closest friend and confidant; the main recipient of his letters.
- Pompey (died 48 BCE): his long-time, if disappointing, political champion.
- Cato (died 46 BCE): an ally in principle, though Cicero found him rigid.
- Marcus Brutus: friend and intellectual partner; Cicero dedicated works to him and named the dialogue Brutus after him.
- Caesar: correct, sometimes cordial personal relations, especially after the pardon, but no political alliance. Caesar admired his literary skill.
- Caesarians who liked him personally: Hirtius, Pansa, Balbus, Oppius and Dolabella (until the divorce).

------------------------------------------------------------
RIVALRIES
------------------------------------------------------------
- Mark Antony: mutual hostility, rooted in Cicero's execution of Antony's stepfather Lentulus in 63 BCE.
- Clodius (died 52 BCE): his worst enemy, who drove him into exile in 58 BCE.
- Caesar, politically: Cicero resented that the Republic now depended on one man's mercy. He wrote praise of Cato; Caesar answered with an Anticato (45 BCE).
- Crassus: uneasy relations, now in the past.
- Cicero mocked Caesar's honours privately (for example, his statue in the temple of Quirinus) and joked bitterly when Caesar made a man consul for only a few hours on 31 December 45 BCE.

------------------------------------------------------------
MILITARY INFLUENCE
------------------------------------------------------------
- Very low. Cicero was a civilian politician.
- His only command: governor of Cilicia (51-50 BCE), where he led a small campaign in the Amanus mountains, was hailed "imperator" by his troops and hoped, unsuccessfully, for a triumph.
- His influence is moral authority, oratory and his network of letters, not soldiers.

------------------------------------------------------------
RELATIONSHIPS WITH OTHER PEOPLE
------------------------------------------------------------
- Julius Caesar: pardoner and occasional guest. In December 45 BCE Caesar visited Cicero's villa near Puteoli with a large escort; Cicero wrote that the visit was pleasant, the talk literary, and Caesar "not a guest to whom you'd say: do come again."
- Marcus Brutus: friend and fellow philosopher.
- Cassius: friendly correspondent, discussing Epicureanism in 45 BCE.
- Mark Antony: enemy.
- Dolabella: former son-in-law; personally charming but unreliable.
- Atticus: closest friend.
- Quintus: brother, with a strained relationship.
- Former Pompeians: he spoke for Marcus Marcellus (46 BCE), Quintus Ligarius (46) and King Deiotarus (November 45) before Caesar.

------------------------------------------------------------
HISTORICAL ACTIONS (to end of 45 BCE)
------------------------------------------------------------
- 106 BCE: born at Arpinum.
- 70 BCE: prosecutes Verres; establishes himself as Rome's leading advocate.
- 63 BCE: consul; suppresses Catiline's conspiracy; executes five conspirators without trial, including Antony's stepfather Lentulus.
- 60-59 BCE: declines to join Caesar, Pompey and Crassus.
- 58-57 BCE: exiled through Clodius's law; recalled after about 18 months.
- 56 BCE: pressured to support the "triumvirs".
- 51-50 BCE: governor of Cilicia.
- 49-48 BCE: joins Pompey in Greece after hesitation; absent from Pharsalus.
- 48-47 BCE: waits at Brundisium; pardoned by Caesar (September 47).
- 46 BCE: speeches for Marcellus and Ligarius; divorces Terentia; marries Publilia; writes Brutus, Orator and Cato.
- February 45 BCE: Tullia dies.
- 45 BCE: writes Academica, On Ends and the Tusculans; defends Deiotarus; hosts Caesar at Puteoli (December).

Sources: Cicero's letters to Atticus (books 12-13) and to friends; Pro Marcello; Pro Ligario; Pro Rege Deiotaro; Plutarch, Life of Cicero.
